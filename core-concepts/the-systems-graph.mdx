A physical system is described by many structures at once: the product breakdown, interfaces, requirements, analyses, tests, parts, risks, and decisions. Each is useful alone. The understanding comes from how they connect. Change one, and the others can move.

Flow keeps all of them connected in a systems graph. The graph answers the questions engineers ask every day: what is this part of, why is it this way, what backs it up, and what else changes if it changes.

## Start from an owned system

Flow is built around ownership. Every item has an accountable person, and that person is the engineer closest to the design. The owner of a subsystem owns its parts, its interfaces to the rest of the product, and the evidence that it works.

The owner still works with specialists and reviewers; ownership gives that collaboration a center, and keeps each part of the model current while the design changes.

Ownership only helps when the owner can see their dependencies. The graph makes them visible.

## Make every connection explicit

Flow represents information as **items** connected by **relationships**. A system, an interface, a requirement, an analysis, a test, or a part are all items, in categories your team defines.

Some relationships express decomposition: a product contains systems, which contain subsystems and parts.

Other relationships cross that hierarchy. A vehicle's range depends on both battery capacity and aerodynamic drag, which belong to different systems and different owners. An interface ties two systems together. A folder tree cannot hold both kinds of connection. A graph can.

Evidence completes the picture. Analyses, tests, and their results connect to what they support. The structure says what the team intends to build. The evidence says how close it is.

## Change the view, not the data

Different jobs ask different questions of the same records. A subsystem owner wants their system and what is still open on it. A systems architect wants interfaces and dependencies. A program lead wants progress across the whole program.

Each gets a view, and every view reads the same items:

- A **table** compares fields across items and edits them in bulk.
- A **tree** shows hierarchy and decomposition.
- **Reader** presents the content as a document people can read and comment on.
- A **diagram** draws selected structures and connections.

Saved views can start from different parts of the graph, such as systems, functions, verification, or risks. Nothing is copied between them. Editing a value in one view changes the item every view shows, and a link to an item opens it in context: focused in the main view, revealed in the navigator, and open in its details.

Views can also read across branches. A table can show a field as another branch holds it, beside the current branch's value, so comparing a proposal with its parent does not mean leaving the branch.

A filtered view is a perspective, not a permission. Access is configured separately.

## Follow the consequences of change

Suppose a target changes. The question is not the new number. It is everything connected to it: the items that depend on it, the budgets it feeds, and the evidence that no longer shows what it did.

The graph turns that question into a set of records to inspect and people to talk to. Its answers are only as good as its links. A missing relationship is a gap in understanding, for engineers and agents alike, and a recorded one still needs engineering judgment.
