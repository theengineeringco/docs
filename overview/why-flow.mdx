title: "Why Flow?"
description: "See how connected information supports engineering decisions, coordination, and review."
---

An engineering change rarely ends with the item being edited. A revised requirement may affect a design, a test, and another team's assumptions. Flow brings the information and its relationships into a shared model so the team can examine those consequences together.

## Follow a decision to its evidence

A requirement is more useful when an engineer can find the design that addresses it and the evidence used to verify it. Flow's relationships make those paths explicit. This is **traceability**: being able to follow the connections between an engineering need, the work that fulfills it, and its verification.

In the [cooling-system example](/overview#an-example-changing-a-cooling-system), a reviewer can follow the operating-temperature requirement to the cooling assembly and its verification test. Missing or outdated evidence becomes a question the team can investigate.

Traceability depends on the relationships and evidence the team maintains. A link provides context; it does not by itself prove that a requirement has been met.

## Understand what a change touches

Connected information gives engineers a starting point for impact analysis: examining what a proposed change could affect. A systems engineer can follow dependencies beyond the edited requirement, while domain engineers assess the consequences for their own work.

For a wider operating range, that review might include the cooling assembly's capacity, its interfaces with surrounding systems, and the conditions covered by the verification test. The team can discuss those questions alongside the proposal before accepting it.

## Coordinate across disciplines

Different roles need different perspectives. A mechanical engineer may compare component properties in a table. A systems engineer may inspect relationships in a diagram. A reviewer may read requirements and supporting explanations in a document view.

These views draw from the same model. Teams can work from a shared item and its history while choosing the presentation that suits their task. Ownership and contextual comments help connect questions to the people and work they concern.

## Develop proposals without losing the agreed state

Branches let engineers develop a proposal separately from its parent. Reviews bring the changes, discussion, and decisions together. When the work is accepted, merging applies it to the parent and preserves its history.

Baselines record a project state at a meaningful point in time. They help teams return to a milestone and compare it with later work. Together, these mechanisms make it possible to distinguish a proposal in progress from an accepted decision.

## Put connected context to work

Flow's AI agents can help find information, analyze project data, and prepare changes. Automations support recurring work, while integrations and the API connect Flow with other tools. Availability and capabilities depend on the project's configuration.

AI-assisted work uses the user's permissions and can be inspected through the same model and review tools. Engineering judgment remains with the team.

Start with a model the team can maintain: meaningful item types, useful relationships, and clear ownership. [Core concepts](/overview/core-concepts) explains how those parts fit together.
